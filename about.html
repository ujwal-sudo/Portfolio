My Journey

I'm Ujwal Mahajan, an AI engineer and final-year Electronics & Telecommunication student at SGGSIE&T, Nanded. If there's a pattern running through my time in college, it's this: get close to things, take them apart, and only then decide what's worth going deep on.

College gave me the room to run that experiment properly — clubs, builds, events, side quests — and one by one, most interests taught me what I needed and stepped aside. One didn't.

Robotics & Early Exploring

Robotics came first. Working in the college's robotics club (RNXG) meant soldering, wiring, breaking things, and occasionally making something move on command. It was my first exposure to systems thinking — sensors, actuators, control, the unglamorous middle where everything actually happens.

It also taught me to organize: I'm a lead organizer of RMAGEDDON 2026, the club's robotics event, which is roughly as much about logistics and people as it is about machines.

Leadership · RNXG Systems Under Pressure

Leading RMAGEDDON 2026 & Systems Engineering

Organizing RMAGEDDON 2026 taught me how technical systems behave outside the lab. When dozens of autonomous and radio-controlled robots enter the arena, physical hardware reliability, battery management, RF interference, and team synchronization matter as much as algorithmic elegance.

Detours

Along the way I picked up and put down a lot of things — photography, videography, football, and a long tail of half-pivots. Each one had a season. I don't pitch any of them as credentials now; they were how I figured out what actually holds my attention (and, just as usefully, what doesn't).

Finding AI

AI was different from the first proper look. Not because it was trendy — because it was the first subject where the deeper I went, the more interesting it got. Language models in particular sit at a strange intersection of everything I'd touched: systems, engineering, and a genuinely open scientific frontier.

So I did it properly: Python, then NLP, then the concepts under the hood — attention, tokenization, training dynamics. Not textbooks-only. Reading about a transformer is one thing; implementing one is another.

Learning by Building

I started where everyone starts — frameworks, libraries, calling APIs. They're good tools. But at some point "it works" stopped being enough, and the interesting question became why it works.

That's the line I've been walking since: use the abstractions until you need to know what's inside them, then go inside. It's why Pythia-160M exists — a small language model built from scratch so that nothing in the stack is a black box — and it's why I build agentic systems with a debugger's eye rather than a demo-day eye.

What's Next

The plan is simple to state: build useful intelligent systems, and understand their internals well enough to know why they fail. Training runs, evaluations, compression, agent reliability — there's a long list, and it's getting longer in the right way.
